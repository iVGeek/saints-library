Agatha is one of the most venerated virgin martyrs of the early Church — a young woman of Catania, Sicily, born around 231, who refused the advances of the Roman prefect Quintianus and suffered for it. The story, preserved in her Acts, tells that Quintianus, seeing her beauty, had her arrested on the charge of being a Christian and proposed to trade her freedom for her love. She answered: "I am a servant of Christ; any other lover is hateful to me." He tortured her, had her breasts cut off — the reason she is shown on a dish in art — and finally she died in prison, either from her wounds or from being rolled on live coals. The ground shook; a fire fell from heaven and consumed the judge's house; the crowd, terrified, cried out for mercy.

## The Patroness of Catania

Her cult spread rapidly through the West. She is named in the Canon of the Mass with Agnes, Lucy, and Cecilia, one of the four virgin martyrs of the Roman liturgy. Her relics, translated from Catania to Constantinople and back, are credited with protecting the city from the eruptions of Etna — Catania built a great basilica over her tomb, and in 1126 her relics were brought back from the city's Muslim rulers. She is invoked against fire, earthquakes, and eruptions, and above all she is the patroness of breast cancer patients and of all women who undergo mastectomy — a patronage that has given new meaning to her ancient suffering in the modern age.
