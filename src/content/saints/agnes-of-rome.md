Agnes is remembered as a girl of thirteen — perhaps the most famous of the Roman virgin martyrs — who died for Christ during the persecution of Diocletian around the year 304. Her story is told by the Fathers of the fourth century, who prized her as the very type of youthful courage. Saint Ambrose devoted a treatise to her; the poet Prudentius sang her martyrdom; and her name is still spoken in the Roman Canon of the Mass, in the company of the greatest saints of the Church.

## The Lamb and the Martyr

Her name — *Agnes* in Latin, related to *agnus*, "lamb" — came to fit her perfectly: the lamb of Christ, pure and offered. The accounts of her death vary in detail but agree in substance. A noble Roman family, she was sought in marriage by a young man of rank; she refused, consecrating herself to Christ. When persecution came, she refused to sacrifice to the gods. The prefect threatened her with the horrors of the brothel; tradition says her purity was protected by miracle, and the sword that was meant to take her life spared her at the executioner's fear — until she yielded her neck to it gladly. She died about the age of thirteen, more radiant, in the words of her legend, than a bride on her wedding day.

## The Basilica and the Lambs

Her tomb on the Via Nomentana outside Rome became one of the great shrines of the early Church, and a basilica was built over it in the fourth century. There, to this day, on her feast day of 21 January, lambs are blessed; their wool is woven into the pallia — the bands of white wool worn by archbishops as a sign of their office and of the Good Shepherd. The little martyr of Rome thus continues, through her feast and her lambs, to witness to the Church that the Lamb who takes away the sins of the world is worth everything, even at thirteen.
