Albert the Great is the rarest of medieval figures: a man who read everything, collected everything, and made the whole physical world the subject of his prayer. Born around 1206 at Lauingen in Swabia, he joined the young Order of Preachers at Padua and spent his life at the crossroads of two floods of knowledge — the newly translated works of Aristotle and the Bible. In an age that feared the first would drown the second, Albert insisted they came from the same Source.

## The Man Who Studied Everything

Albert's learning was staggering. He wrote on logic, physics, astronomy, alchemy, minerals, plants, and animals, watching the behavior of bees and recording what he saw; he wrote commentaries on Aristotle that became the textbooks of Europe; and he wrote theology that drew all of it into the service of the faith. To the Middle Ages he was "the Great" and "the Universal Doctor," the living proof that the naturalist and the Christian are not rivals. He is today the patron of scientists — a title, the Church says, that means the laboratory and the sanctuary are one room.

## The Master of Thomas

His greatest monument is his student. When Thomas Aquinas arrived in Paris, heavy and silent, Albert answered the mockers who called him an ox: "This ox will one day low so loudly the whole world will hear." At Cologne, Albert built the school where Thomas learned to reconcile reason and revelation, and the friendship of the two Dominicans became the partnership on which Catholic intellectual life still rests. Albert served his Church too — bishop of Regensburg against his will, preacher, reformer — but he always returned to his books and his students. He died at Cologne in 1280; his tomb is still a place of pilgrimage, and the "dumb ox" stands today as the proof of his master's prophecy.
