Ambrose is the Church's proof that God drafts whom He chooses. In 374 the see of Milan fell vacant, and the assembly met to elect a bishop — a mob ready to fight, Arians and Catholics at each other's throats. The imperial governor, a layman named Ambrose, came into the church to keep the peace; and suddenly, the whole crowd — the hagiographers swear even the children — shouted his name. He fled, he protested that he was not even baptized, he was confined and baptized and ordained, and within eight days, the lawyer who had never wanted the job was the most powerful bishop of the West.

## The Choir of Milan

Ambrose made the episcopate a throne of eloquence. In his cathedral he taught the people by preaching and by singing, writing the Latin hymns — *Te Deum*, the ambrosian chants — that still give the liturgy its wings. It was his singing and his preaching that drew a restless young African rhetorician named Augustine into the church, and in 387 Ambrose baptized Augustine — the teacher of the age receiving baptism from the bishop who had captured his heart. The bee that once settled on the infant Ambrose's lips without stinging was the Church's early symbol of the sweetness of his speech.

## The Shepherd Who Stood

His courage was the courage of the father who must sometimes correct the king. When the Empress Justina demanded a basilica for the Arians, Ambrose refused and let the people fill the church to hold it. When the Emperor Theodosius massacred the people of Thessalonica, Ambrose barred the doors of the cathedral and refused the emperor communion until he had done public penance — and Theodosius submitted. "He was a bishop of God's making," wrote his biographer. The moon he preached about, bright with a borrowed light, was the image of a Church that shines not by its own glory but by the sun of its Lord — and Ambrose was its clearest mirror in the West.
