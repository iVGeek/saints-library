The saint whom millions call simply "Saint Anthony" never intended to be the patron of lost items — the most famous devotion to him arose from a story of a stolen book. Born Fernando de Bulhões in Lisbon in 1195, he was the son of a noble Portuguese family and entered religious life as a young Augustinian canon. A visit from the relics of five Franciscan martyrs, freshly slain in Morocco, changed his course: he joined the Friars Minor in 1220, taking the name Anthony after the great desert abbot.

## Preacher and Teacher

For the first months of his Franciscan life he was assigned to the kitchen and the roads, living quietly among the brothers. His gifts emerged almost by accident. At a gathering of Franciscan preachers, when no one could be found to speak, Anthony was asked to give the sermon. Those who heard him that day recognized a preacher of astonishing power — learned, eloquent, and aflame with love of the gospel.

Saint Francis himself, hearing of it, gave him the office of teaching theology to the brothers, calling him "my bishop." Yet Anthony remained always a preacher to the people. For the last years of his short life he traveled the cities of northern Italy and southern France, drawing crowds that sometimes filled the open squares for days, and was known for his defense of the poor and his fearless preaching against the errors of his time.

## The Infant Jesus and the Lost Book

Tradition says that while Anthony was praying at the house of a nobleman in Camposanpiero, the Infant Jesus appeared to him in his arms — and it is thus, holding the Christ Child, that he is most often portrayed. Another well-loved story tells of a novice who took away his psalter; Anthony prayed, and the novice saw a frightful vision and returned the book. It is from this tale that he became the patron of lost items, though the devotion, with its gentle trust, has spread to all things lost — keys, papers, hopes, and faith itself.

## The Lightning Canonization

Anthony died at Padua on 13 June 1231, at only thirty-five. His funeral was the scene of reported miracles, and Pope Gregory IX canonized him less than a year later — one of the swiftest canonizations in history. In 1946 Pope Pius XII declared him a Doctor of the Church, honoring a preacher whose sermons still ring with the clarity of the gospel. His tongue, venerated incorrupt at Padua, stands as a symbol of a life spent entirely in speaking the Word.
