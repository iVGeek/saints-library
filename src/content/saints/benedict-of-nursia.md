Benedict of Nursia never intended to found an order. He intended only to seek God, but the simple rule of life he wrote for a group of monks in the mountains of central Italy became the blueprint of Western monasticism — and, in the judgement of historians, one of the formative influences on the civilization of Europe.

## Flight from the World

Born around 480 at Nursia, in the mountains northeast of Rome, Benedict was sent to Rome for his studies as a youth of good family. He found the capital of the fallen empire corrupt and its schools decadent, and he fled — abandoning his books, his servant, and his inheritance — to live as a hermit in a cave at Subiaco. His holiness drew disciples. Around him gathered groups of monks, and in the valley of Subiaco he founded twelve small monasteries. About 529, perhaps fleeing hostile local clergy, he moved south to the summit of Monte Cassino, midway between Rome and Naples, where he destroyed a pagan shrine and founded the abbey that became the mother house of Western monasticism.

## The Rule

At Monte Cassino Benedict composed his Rule: seventy-three short chapters of astonishing balance and humanity. It orders every hour of the monk's day — prayer, work, reading, rest — around a single principle: "Let nothing be preferred to the Work of God." Its prologue begins, "Listen carefully, my child, to the master's instructions, and attend to them with the ear of your heart." It is a rule of moderation: enough food, enough sleep, and enough work to serve God joyfully. The motto *Ora et labora* — "pray and work" — has come to sum up its spirit.

## A Civilizing Legacy

The monasteries that followed the Rule became the great conservators of Western learning: copying manuscripts, farming the land, keeping hospitality, and educating the young. When the barbarian kingdoms were forming the nations of Europe, the Benedictine abbeys were islands of prayer and culture. For this the Church has honored Benedict as co-patron of Europe, and Pope Saint John Paul II named him patron of the continent's students. His feast is kept on July 11; his medal, with its cross and its prayer against evil, remains one of the most used sacramentals in the Catholic world.
