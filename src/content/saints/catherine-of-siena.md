Catherine Benincasa, the twenty-fourth child of a Sienese dyer, was neither a nun nor a scholar, yet she became one of the most influential women in the history of the Church — a lay mystic who wrote to popes, called for the reform of the Church, and, centuries after her death, was made a Doctor of the Church.

## The Young Mystic of Siena

Born in 1347, Catherine was a bright and strong-willed child who told her mother at six that she had consecrated her virginity to Christ. Against the plans of her family for her marriage, she cut off her hair and devoted herself to prayer and penance, becoming a Dominican tertiary — a lay member of the Order of Penance — in her teens. For three years she lived in seclusion in a tiny cell, then emerged to a life of extraordinary service among the sick, the poor, the condemned, and the plague-stricken of Siena.

## Counselor of Popes

Catherine's influence reached the highest levels of Christendom. She dictated hundreds of letters — to popes, kings, cardinals, and sinners — urging reform, peace, and the return of the papacy from Avignon to Rome. In 1376 she traveled to Avignon herself and spoke face to face with Pope Gregory XI, who soon returned to Rome. When the Western Schism tore the Church apart in 1378, Catherine gave her life in intercession for its unity.

## The Fire of the Soul

Her great spiritual work, *The Dialogue*, is a conversation between the soul and God on the theme of divine providence and love. In it, God tells her: "I am He who is; you are she who is not." Her mysticism was intensely bodily and intensely practical: she received the stigmata (visible, tradition says, only to her confessor), yet spent her days nursing the sick and washing the sores of the poor. She died in Rome in 1380, at thirty-three, worn out by prayer and labor. In 1970 Pope Paul VI proclaimed her a Doctor of the Church — the first laywoman to receive the title. With Saint Francis she shares the patronage of Italy, and with Saint Bridget and Saint Teresa Benedicta of the Cross the patronage of Europe.
