Cecilia is the patroness of music, and the whole world knows her by that office — the saint of the opera houses and the choral societies, whose feast on November 22 is still a festival of church music. But the historical heart of her legend is stranger and stronger than any aria. A Roman noblewoman of the third century, she was married against her will to a young nobleman named Valerian, and on her wedding night, as the musicians played at the feast, "she sang in her heart to the Lord": *may my heart be undefiled, that I be not confounded.* She had consecrated her virginity to Christ and was determined to keep it even in marriage.

## The Conversion of Valerian

Her wedding night became a mission. She told Valerian of her vow and of the angel who guarded her, and Valerian — instead of anger — asked to be made worthy to see the angel. He sought out Pope Urban, was baptized, and came home a Christian; his brother Tiburtius was converted with him. The two brothers distributed alms, buried the martyrs, and were themselves seized and beheaded for the faith. Cecilia buried them, and when she was arrested in her turn, she confessed Christ before the prefect with such eloquence that her judge remarked he had never met a woman who spoke so boldly.

## The Song That Would Not End

Condemned to die, she was shut in the steam-room of her own house to suffocate; legend says she sang through it. When the executioner came with the sword, he struck her three times on the neck but could not kill her, and she lingered three days, giving her goods to the poor and her blessing to those who came to her. She died, and was buried in the catacombs of Callistus. In the ninth century her body was brought to the church built over her house in Trastevere — the church of Santa Cecilia — and in 1599 her tomb was opened and the saint found as she had lain, incorrupt, her neck marked by the sword. The sculptor who beheld her sketched what he saw, and that image — the martyr who sang with her heart, crowned by the music of heaven — is the Cecilia the world remembers and the one the Church honors as its patroness of song.
