Charles Borromeo was a young aristocrat made suddenly powerful: at twenty-two his uncle, Pope Pius IV, made him a cardinal, and at twenty-six archbishop of Milan and papal secretary of state. Other men might have dined out on such good fortune; Charles spent it. At the last sessions of the Council of Trent, still in his twenties, he was one of the draftsmen of the reform that would reshape the Church; and when his elder brother died in 1562, he chose — over the glitter of Rome — to resign his curial honors and go home to be a bishop.

## The Bishop with a Pen and a Walking Stick

Milan in the 1560s was a scandal of absentee bishops and worldly clergy. Charles returned to it and simply began walking: up into the Alps, down through the plain, visiting every parish on foot, preaching, catechizing, holding councils. He founded seminaries when there were almost none, wrote the catechisms that educated Europe, and insisted that his clergy be worthy of their altars — reformers under him said he was a "hammer of the simoniacs." When a member of the Humiliati order tried to assassinate him in 1569, he prayed for the man and went on with the work.

## In the Time of the Plague

The plague of 1576 found him at the perfect test. While the rich fled the city, Charles stayed, gave away his goods, organized the hospitals, buried the dead with his own hands, and spent himself among the dying until the crisis passed. He died worn out in 1584 at forty-six, and the Church canonized him in 1610. His legacy is the seminary, the catechism class, the parish that actually has a bishop in it — the Reformation of the Church accomplished, not by argument, but by a pastor who lived what he preached.
