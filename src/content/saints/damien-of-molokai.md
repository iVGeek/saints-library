When the Hawaiian government decided in 1865 to confine every person with leprosy to a settlement on the island of Molokai, it banished them not only from the islands but from the human race. Kalaupapa, the settlement, sat under a thousand-foot cliff, ringed by the sea, where the sick were landed and left — to build what shelters they could from poles and rags, to die without priest, doctor, or dignity. For years no priest would stay with them. The mission sent volunteers; they came, said Mass, and left. And then in 1873 a Belgian brother of the Congregation of the Sacred Hearts, thirty-three years old, arrived to offer a few weeks of his service and stayed for the rest of his life.

## "I Make Myself a Leper with the Leper"

Damien de Veuster — born Joseph at Tremelo in 1840, who had left a farm for the missions and been ordained in Honolulu — walked among the lepers of Kalaupapa as one of them. He learned their language, called them "my brothers," built them a church and a hospital, dug their graves with his own hands. When he found the settlement's water tainted, he carried fresh water from the mountains; when the dead were buried in shallow graves, he deepened them himself. His letters are full of the practical arithmetic of charity: coffins, roofs, children to be taught, the dying to be held. He did not preach at them; he lived among them, ate with them, and in 1884 — eleven years after he arrived — a doctor told him the news he had long expected: he had leprosy himself.

## The Saint of the Outcasts

He was at once the most famous and the most accused priest of his day. The newspapers of the world made him a hero; the critics asked, cruelly, whether he had caught the disease from his people because he had been too close. He answered from Kalaupapa, where he worked on until he could not stand, saying only: "I make myself a leper with the lepers to gain all to Jesus Christ." He died on 15 April 1889, at forty-nine, and his body was buried — despite his request to stay among his people — in Belgium, and later returned to Molokai. On 11 October 2009 Benedict XVI canonized him, the Church's answer to the century that had doubted him: a man who served the excluded until he was counted among them, and who is now invoked by the sick, the outcast, and all who carry the world's most feared diseases.
