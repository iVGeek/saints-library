Elizabeth of Hungary had everything a princess could want — and she gave it all away so quickly that her court called her mad. The daughter of King Andrew II of Hungary, she was married at fourteen to Louis IV of Thuringia in a rare medieval match of true love; her mother-in-law had to order the castle gates opened to her twice-daily distributions of bread. But Elizabeth's charity was no gesture of the great lady. She had a hospital built at the foot of the Wartburg, and there, in the rags of the sick, she washed their wounds, dressed their sores, and laid the dead out for burial — with the crown of Thuringia still on her head.

## The Queen and the Roses

The most famous legend of her life grew from the marriage of her love and her poverty: her husband, coming upon her once with her apron full of bread for the poor, chided her, and when he opened the apron to see what she hid, it was full of roses — roses in winter, so the story says. It is a fairytale the Middle Ages told of a real queen. When the landgrave's soldiers searched her, the loaves she carried became roses; when the bishops who distrusted her austerity brought her to court, the bread on her plate turned to flowers. Beneath the legend is the truth: a queen for whom the poor were not an inconvenience but the presence of Christ.

## The Widow and the Habit

In 1227 her world broke. Louis died of plague on the way to the Crusade, and Elizabeth was driven from the Wartburg with her children — in winter, they said, without a coat. Her confessor, the severe Master Conrad, held her to the letter of obedience; her in-laws took her children; and this girl of twenty, once queen, built herself a hut of clay at Marburg and took the gray habit of Saint Francis. There she founded a hospital and served the dying until she died, in 1231, at twenty-four. She was canonized within four years — one of the fastest canonizations in history — because the poor she served were still alive to say what they had seen: a queen who had loved them, and washed them, and stayed with them to the end.
