Francis Bernardone, the son of a wealthy cloth merchant of Assisi, began his life as a bright young man of the town — fond of poetry, fine clothes, and the glamour of the medieval Italian city-states. His conversion, which unfolded through war, imprisonment, illness, and encounter with the poor, became the most beloved story of sanctity in the Western Church.

## The Conversion

As a young man Francis longed for knighthood. In 1202 he rode off to battle against Perugia, was captured, and spent a year as a prisoner in a damp cistern. Sickness followed, and with it the slow awakening of his soul. He began to pray in the abandoned chapel of San Damiano outside Assisi, and there, according to his first biographers, the painted crucifix spoke to him: "Francis, go and repair my house, which, as you see, is falling into ruins." Francis took the words literally, selling cloth from his father's warehouse to restore the little church.

His father, furious, brought him before the bishop's court. In one of the most famous scenes of Christian history, Francis stripped himself of his rich clothing before the bishop and his townsfolk, declaring that henceforth God alone was his Father. He walked out into the Umbrian countryside a poor man, wearing a rough tunic, his hands free for God.

## Brother Francis

His way of life soon attracted companions. The Friars Minor — "lesser brothers" — lived by the gospel: owning nothing, begging their bread, and preaching repentance and joy. In 1210 Francis traveled to Rome and won the oral approval of Pope Innocent III for a simple rule of life. The Franciscan movement spread with remarkable speed, and in 1212 the young noblewoman Clare of Assisi fled her home to embrace the same poverty, giving rise to the Poor Clares.

Francis was a deacon, not a priest, and he described himself always as "the little poor man." His love extended to all creation: he called the sun "Brother," the moon "Sister," and is said to have preached to the birds and made peace between a wolf and the town of Gubbio. These stories are told in the tradition with devotion and are understood as signs of his profound harmony with God's creation — a harmony the Church later honored by naming him patron of ecology.

## The Holy Land and the Stigmata

In 1219 Francis traveled to the East and, at Damietta during the Fifth Crusade, walked unarmed into the camp of the Sultan al-Kamil to speak of Christ. He did not convert the Sultan, but he was treated with respect and returned safely — an early and remarkable gesture of Christian–Muslim encounter carried out in the spirit of peace.

At Christmas 1223, at Greccio, Francis arranged the first living nativity scene, with a manger, an ox, and an ass, so that the faithful might contemplate the humility of the Incarnation. In September 1224, while praying on Mount La Verna, he received the stigmata — the wounds of Christ impressed upon his hands, feet, and side.

## The Canticle and the Good Death

Illness and increasing blindness marked his last years. Yet his joy never failed. In his final illness he composed the Canticle of the Creatures, praising God through Brother Sun, Sister Moon, and Sister Death herself. He died on the evening of 3 October 1226, at the little chapel of the Porziuncola, singing his canticle. Two years later, Pope Gregory IX — who had once opposed him and later defended him — canonized him.

His legacy is vast: a family of religious orders, a literature of spiritual joy, a devotion to the Crucified, and a vision of the world as a gift to be received with gratitude rather than grasped. The "Poor Man of Assisi" remains, eight centuries later, one of the most universally loved figures in the history of the Church.
