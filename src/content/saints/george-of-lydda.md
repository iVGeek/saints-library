George is the most famous soldier-saint of the Church — a Roman officer of the late third century who refused to offer sacrifice to the emperors and died for Christ at Lydda, in the Holy Land, during the persecution of Diocletian. Beyond that bare outline, history is frugal, and legend has been lavish. The Church, keeping his feast on April 23, honors the certain fact of his martyrdom and leaves the rest to the poetry that has made him the guardian knight of half of Christendom.

## The Soldier and the Dragon

The story everyone knows is the one the Middle Ages embroidered into glory. A dragon was ravaging a city, and the terrified citizens offered it their children by lot until only the princess remained. George, riding past, made the sign of the cross, struck the beast down, and led it captive into the city — conquered, says the old reading, not by his spear but by faith. For all its charm, the legend is a parable dressed in armor: the dragon is the evil that devours the innocent, the princess is the soul, and the knight who fights for her is every Christian who stands between wrong and the weak. The image of George — the red cross on the white banner — became the emblem of Christian knighthood, of the crusading ideal, and finally of England herself.

## A Saint for Every Battle

Across the Christian world George has been claimed as protector: by soldiers, by horsemen and farmers, by England, by Aragon and Georgia, by the Byzantine emperors before them. His feast on April 23 has been a national holiday of England since the Middle Ages. What survives of the historical man is enough: a Christian officer who, offered promotion, wealth, and life itself to deny his Lord, answered by laying down his commission and his life. That is the core the legends grew around — and it is the reason his banner still flies wherever courage in a good cause is honored.
