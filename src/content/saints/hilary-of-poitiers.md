Hilary of Poitiers is called the "Athanasius of the West" and the "Malleus Arianorum" — the hammer of the Arians — for his defense of the Nicene faith in the fourth century. Born at Poitiers to a pagan family, he converted to Christianity, was elected bishop around 350, and immediately became one of the most formidable theological minds of the age. His great work, On the Trinity, written around 356, defends the divinity of Christ with a precision that earned him the title of Doctor of the Church — and the enmity of the Arian bishops who dominated the emperor's court.

## Exile for the Faith

The emperor Constantius II, a supporter of Arianism, summoned the council of Rimini in 359 and compelled the Western bishops to sign an ambiguous formula that denied the full divinity of the Son. Hilary refused, and for this he was exiled to Phrygia, in the heart of the East — where the Arians expected him to disappear. Instead he debated them on their own ground, confounded their theologians, and won admirers among the very bishops who had exiled him. The Eastern court, exasperated, sent him home. He returned to Poitiers and governed his diocese until his death in 367.

## The Father of the West

Hilary's influence outlived him by centuries. His teaching on the Trinity was received by the Church as authoritative, and his hymns — among the earliest Latin hymns of the Western liturgy — are still sung today. He is the patron of teachers and students, and his feast on January 13, at the beginning of the Christian year, is the feast of a man who gave the Church the courage to say, with the Nicene creed, that the Son is "God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God" — a formulation Hilary fought to preserve, and for which he was willing to lose everything.
