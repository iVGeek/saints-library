James the Greater is the apostle of the sword and the pilgrim — the first of the Twelve to die for Christ, and the apostle whose shrine at Compostela in Spain became the greatest pilgrimage destination of the Middle Ages. Born at Bethsaida, the son of the fisherman Zebedee and of Salome, he was called with his brother John from their boat on the Sea of Galilee, and Jesus gave them the nickname "sons of thunder" — a name that suggests the energy and fierceness of the two young men.

## The Son of Thunder

James was one of the innermost circle of the apostles — present at the Transfiguration, in the garden of Gethsemane, at the raising of Jairus' daughter. He and John asked Jesus for the places of honor in His kingdom, and Jesus warned them: "Can you drink the cup that I am going to drink?" They said they could, and they did — John drank the cup of the cross and lived to old age; James drank it first, in 44 AD, when Herod Agrippa I had him beheaded in Jerusalem. He was the first of the apostles to be martyred, and the account in the Acts of the Apostles says simply that "Herod stretched forth his hands to afflict certain members of the Church, and he killed James, the brother of John, with the sword."

## The Road to Compostela

His relics were lost for centuries, until tradition says they were discovered around 830 in a field in Galicia, Spain, where a hermit saw lights in the sky. The city that grew up around the discovery — Compostela — became the great goal of medieval pilgrimage, the "Way of Saint James" drawing millions across Europe. The scallop shell is his badge, the pilgrim's staff his gift, and the road to Compostela still winds through the hills of France and Spain. Spain adopted him as patron — "Santiago!" — and on his feast, July 25, the whole peninsula remembers the apostle who first brought the Gospel to the West and who still walks the roads, as he did in life, toward the place where Christ waits.
