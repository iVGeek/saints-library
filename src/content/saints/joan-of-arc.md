The story of Joan of Arc is the strangest and most moving in medieval history: an illiterate peasant girl of nineteen who led an army, crowned a king, and then was condemned to the fire by an ecclesiastical court — only to be vindicated, canonized, and made the patroness of her nation. No saint's history is more entangled with the politics of its age; none more clearly shows the Church judging its own judgment.

## The Voices

Joan was born about 1412 at Domrémy, a village on the eastern border of France, in the midst of the Hundred Years' War. At thirteen she began to hear voices — of Saint Michael the Archangel, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret — urging her to save France and to have the Dauphin crowned at Reims. She had no schooling in such things; her faith, like her courage, came from the life of her village. In 1429, after many rebuffs, she made her way to Chinon and, in a scene that has entered legend, picked out the Dauphin Charles from among his courtiers though she had never seen him.

## The Maid of Orleans

Given a small army and a standard painted with Christ and his angels, Joan rode to Orleans, then under siege. Within days the English siege was lifted — the turning point of the war. Charles marched to Reims and was crowned king, with Joan in armor beside the altar. She wished to return home, but the war continued. In May 1430 she was captured at Compiègne by Burgundian forces and sold to the English, who wanted above all to discredit the king she had crowned.

## The Trial and the Fire

At Rouen she was tried by an ecclesiastical court dominated by her political enemies. She was questioned for months — about her voices, her armor, her virginity, her refusal to submit her judgments to the court. The trial was a travesty of canon law; her "answers" were edited, her witnesses packed. Condemned as a relapsed heretic, she was burned at the stake on 30 May 1431, calling on Jesus to the last.

Twenty-five years later, a retrial ordered by Pope Callistus III overturned the verdict as "fraud, defamation, iniquity, and manifest error." Joan was declared innocent. In 1920 Pope Benedict XV canonized her, and she became patroness of France — the peasant girl who trusted her voices, went to the fire rather than deny them, and in death disarmed her judges forever.
