The Piedmont of the 1840s, where factories were swallowing villages, was drowning in abandoned children. Into that world came John Bosco, a priest from a farm family, ordained in 1841, who one Sunday met a boy in the sacristy of his church and — by refusing to scold him — won him. From that first boy grew the oratories of Valdocco, and from the oratories grew a way of education that has circled the globe.

## Reason, Religion, Kindness

Don Bosco's secret, as he preached it to his Salesians, was a triangle: reason, religion, and loving kindness. He would not run reformatories for the young; he ran playgrounds. He fed his boys, taught them trades, found them work, and told them that God loved them — that was the whole curriculum. He defied the prison-like schools of his day by simply never striking a boy, and he filled the long hours with games, bands, and the printed word. A printer himself by training, he poured out books and catechisms for the poor, and his dream of a worldwide mission for youth reached even to the plains of Argentina before his death.

## The Dreamer of Heaven

John Bosco's life was marked by extraordinary dreams, and the most famous of them all — a vision of boys, playing and praying, beneath a city of light — was his charter: heaven, he believed, is the home the young are made for, and his job was to walk them there. On the evening of his death in 1888, he asked the boys to come back the next day for the "good night" word, and then — with "Tell the boys I am waiting for them in paradise" — he let go. The Church canonized him in 1934, and his feast falls at the end of January, a patron for every classroom, every workshop, and every parent who has ever been told that love is not enough — until it is.
