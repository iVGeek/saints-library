John Fisher was a bishop in the mold of Thomas Becket — a man of learning who died not for refusing to bow to the king but for refusing to bow to the king's claim over the Church. Born at Beverley in 1469, he became a Fellow of Queens' College, Cambridge, and rose to the bishopric of Rochester, the poorest diocese in England, which he served for thirty-one years. He was a scholar of the first rank, a preacher, a patron of Erasmus, and a confessor of the faith who, when Henry VIII demanded the oath of supremacy acknowledging himself head of the Church, refused and was imprisoned in the Tower of London.

## The Bishop in the Tower

For fourteen months Fisher sat in the Tower, stripped of his goods, denied visitors, and finally starved. He was made a cardinal by Clement VII, and Henry, in a fury, ordered that the red hat be hung on Fisher's shoulders as he went to the scaffold — an act of spite the bishop met with quiet gratitude. On 22 June 1535, worn to skin and bone, he was led to Tower Hill and executed for the crime of denying the king's spiritual supremacy. His last words were: "I die in the faith of Christ's Holy Catholic Church, and I trust to be saved by the merits of the blood of Jesus Christ." His head was stuck on London Bridge, and there it hung for two weeks, until the king grew tired of looking at it.

## The Companion of More

He was canonized in 1935 with Thomas More, his friend and fellow martyr, who went to the scaffold eleven days after Fisher. Together they are the English Church's answer to every age that has asked whether truth is worth dying for — and the answer of two men of genius who died in the same summer, on the same hill, for the same principle: that no king, however powerful, can claim the authority that belongs to Christ alone.
