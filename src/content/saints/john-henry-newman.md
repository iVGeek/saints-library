John Henry Newman was the most famous convert of Victorian England — an Oxford don who gave up a brilliant Anglican career to follow his conscience into the Catholic Church, and who, in the twentieth century, was raised to the altars of that Church as a saint. His life spans the whole movement of modern thought: the birth of the university, the crisis of faith, and the renewal of Catholic life in the English-speaking world.

## The Oxford Scholar

Born in London in 1801, Newman was a gifted and serious student who found his vocation at Oxford. As an Anglican priest and fellow of Oriel College, he became the leader of the Oxford Movement — a campaign to recover the apostolic faith and sacramental life of the Church of England. His sermons at Saint Mary's Oxford drew crowds of students. His lifelong companion was a poem, begun during an illness on the Mediterranean in 1833: "Lead, kindly Light, amid the encircling gloom — lead Thou me on!"

## The Long Journey to Rome

The more Newman studied the history of the Church, the more he became convinced that the Church of England could not stand against it — and in 1845, after years of agonized study, he was received into the Catholic Church at Littlemore, near Oxford. "I have not sinned against the light," he wrote. Ordained a priest in Rome, he joined the Oratorians and founded the Oratory at Birmingham. When Charles Kingsley accused him of dishonesty, Newman answered with the *Apologia Pro Vita Sua* — a spiritual autobiography that ranks with Augustine's *Confessions*. In 1864 he had also founded the Catholic University of Ireland, whose essays became *The Idea of a University*.

## Cardinal and Saint

Pope Leo XIII made him a cardinal in 1879. In his cardinal's address he gave a motto to the modern conscience: "Cor ad cor loquitur" — heart speaks to heart. He died on 11 August 1890, and for a century his influence only grew: he anticipated the Second Vatican Council's teaching on scripture, conscience, and the laity. Pope Benedict XVI beatified him in 2010, and Pope Francis canonized him in 2019 — an Oxford don and a searcher for truth, now honored as a saint by the Church he loved.
