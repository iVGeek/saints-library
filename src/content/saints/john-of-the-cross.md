John of the Cross wrote the most beautiful poetry of God in the Spanish language, and he wrote it from the bottom of a prison. He was born in 1542 at Fontiveros, the son of a weaver who had been cast out of his noble family for marrying a poor woman, and he grew up in a house where there was often no bread. His father died when John was small; his mother carried the family by her loom. The boy who rose from that poverty became a Carmelite, a reformer, a mystic — and the man who, at the height of his powers, was seized by his own unreformed brothers and thrown into a dungeon at Toledo, beaten and left in the dark.

## The Dark Night

It was in that prison that he composed, on scraps of paper smuggled out between meals, the beginning of his *Spiritual Canticle* — a love song between the soul and God. Nine months later he escaped through a window, let himself down into the night, and fled on foot to a convent of the nuns he had helped to found. In the years that followed he wrote the works that make him the Church's master of mystical theology: *The Ascent of Mount Carmel*, *The Dark Night of the Soul*, *The Living Flame of Love*. His teaching is the strangest and most hopeful in Christian literature: that God is found not by accumulating good feelings but by letting everything else fall away — the "dark night" in which the soul, stripped of every consoling image, is finally led by faith alone into the burning heart of God.

## The Friend of Teresa

He had been recruited to the reform by Teresa of Ávila herself — the meeting in 1567 when the fifty-two-year-old foundress persuaded the twenty-five-year-old friar to join her project, and the friendship of the two Carmelite reformers remade the order. He founded the first house of the Discalced Carmelites at Duruelo, a hovel with a cross of branches; he served as confessor, rector, provincial; and through it all he kept the austerity of a man who truly believed that to lose all was to gain all. He died at Úbeda in 1591, worn out and blamed by his own superiors, in a cell whose window he could not see. His last words were a verse from his own dark-night poem: "Into the night I go, unclothed and unhurried, to meet the fire." He was canonized in 1726 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1926.
