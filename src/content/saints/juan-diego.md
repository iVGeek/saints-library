On 9 December 1531, an Aztec convert named Cuauhtlatoatzin — baptized ten years before as Juan Diego — was walking over the hill of Tepeyac, outside Mexico City, on his way to Sunday Mass. He was fifty-seven, a widower, a simple farmer and weaver of cactus fiber; ten years earlier he had belonged to the empire that was now Spanish. On the hill he heard birdsong and a voice that called his name, and he climbed to meet the woman the voice belonged to: a girl "whose clothing shone like the sun," who stood on the hilltop with the stars at her feet. She told him she was the Mother of God, and she asked for a church on that hill, where the gods of his own people had once been worshiped and where she would now show her love to all her children.

## The Messenger Who Would Not Be Believed

Juan Diego did what any of us would do: he went to the bishop, Fray Juan de Zumárraga, and repeated the message. The bishop, kind but wary, asked for a sign. Juan Diego returned to the hill, and twice the Lady sent him back — the second time to a dying uncle, José María, whom she healed, saying "Call me by the name of the Immaculate, the Ever-Virgin Mary, for it is I, she who will heal his affliction." Then she told him to gather the roses blooming on the frozen hill in December and carry them in his tilma, his cloak of cactus fiber, and take them as the sign the bishop wanted.

## The Tilma of Tepeyac

When Juan Diego opened his cloak before the bishop, the winter roses spilled out — and on the tilma itself was the image of the Lady: the dark-haired, brown-skinned woman, clothed in the colors of the dawn, standing on the moon. The bishop fell to his knees. The image remains today, four and a half centuries later, on the same woven cactus cloth that should have rotted away — studied and photographed, defied by every law of fabric and pigment, and honored by millions. Juan Diego lived out his life as the keeper of the shrine on Tepeyac, "small, poor, simple," and in 2002 John Paul II canonized him at the very shrine — the first indigenous saint of the Americas, and the proof that God chooses, not the powerful, but the little ones who say yes.
