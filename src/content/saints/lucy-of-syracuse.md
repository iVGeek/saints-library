Lucy's name is the story. Her parents called her Lucia, from the Latin *lux* — light — and she came into the world, so the ancient Acts tell, as the light that fills the darkness of the shortest days. She was born at Syracuse in Sicily toward the end of the third century, a girl of noble family, and she had consecrated herself to God before she was out of her teens. When her mother fell dangerously ill, Lucy persuaded her to make pilgrimage to the tomb of Saint Agatha, and there her mother was healed. In her gratitude she gave Lucy what the world considered her greatest treasure: permission to live as she had vowed, poor and pure, and she gave her dowry away to the poor.

## The Gifts of Fire

The rejected suitor, angry at losing both the girl and her fortune, denounced her to the pagan governor as a Christian — and in the persecution of Diocletian, that was a death sentence. The Acts tell the legend in flames: when the governor ordered her taken to a brothel, she was immovable, heavy as a mountain; when they tried to burn her, the fire would not take; when they struck her with the sword, she received the blow and died praising God. The medieval painters put the sword at her throat and her eyes — torn out, the later legend said, and given back to her whole — on a dish, and in Sweden and Italy, on the long night of December 13, girls in white crowns of candles still carry her light through the dark.

## The Light of the Shortest Day

The Church sets Lucy's feast at the turning of the year, when the northern sun is at its lowest — the festival of light at the heart of winter. She stands in the Canon of the Mass with Agnes and Agatha, one of the women "consecrated to Christ in the west," and she is invoked by all whose eyes grow dim and all who wait in darkness. What the world has always loved in her is not merely the legend but the truth: a girl who owned little, gave all, and looked straight at the sword because she could see, farther on, a Light that no night could put out.
