Martin de Porres was born in Lima in 1579, the son of a Spanish knight who refused at first to acknowledge him and of Ana de Velázquez, a freed black woman. He grew up poor and of mixed race in a Spanish colony that graded human worth by blood, and he spent his life quietly dismantling that ledger with a broom and a basin of water. From boyhood he begged to enter the Dominican priory of the Holy Rosary; barred by the color of his skin from the ranks of the choir religious, he served as a lay brother — and made service itself his throne.

## The Infirmarian of Lima

Martin became the infirmarian of his priory, and through him the convent doors swung open to the sick of Lima without regard to rank or race. He dressed wounds, made medicines, fed the hungry from the friars' own table, and took in orphans and abandoned children. His charity reached beyond the human family: the same tenderness he showed the poor he showed the stray cats and dogs of the city, founding for them the first animal shelter of the Americas. Legend, generous with him, tells of miracles of healing and of being seen in two places at once — the soul, so to speak, outrunning his humble body.

## The Broom of Holiness

The hagiographers always picture Martin with a broom — not because he was trapped in a servant's place, but because he chose it and sanctified it. When his superiors offered him the honors his birth now permitted, he asked instead to be left in the humblest duties. His holiness is the Church's great counter-testimony to the racisms of every age: the son of a slave and a knight, kneeling to wash the feet of the poor, is the saint canonized in 1962, in the same year the Church called the whole world to open its eyes to the dignity of every race. His feast on November 3 reminds us that sanctity is color-blind — and that the broom in God's house is the brightest of crowns.
