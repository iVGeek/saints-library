The story that made Martin of Tours the most famous saint of the Western Church in his own lifetime is a story of a coat. As a young cavalryman in the Roman army, stationed at Amiens, he met at the city gate a beggar shivering in rags. Martin drew his sword, cut his own military cloak in two, and gave half to the poor man. That night Christ appeared to him in a dream, clothed in the half-cloak, saying to the angels: "Martin, who is only a catechumen, has clothed Me in this garment." The scene — soldier, beggar, cloak, and Christ — became the emblem of the whole Christian West.

## From the Army to the Altar

Martin was born in Pannonia around 316, the son of a veteran, and at fifteen was forced into the army he hated. He was still a catechumen when he gave the cloak away; shortly after, baptized, he asked his commander for leave to serve another king: "I am Christ's soldier; I am not allowed to fight." Charged with cowardice, he offered to stand unarmed before the enemy line in proof of his faith; the battle was called off, and he went free — the first conscientious objector of the Church to be crowned a saint. He followed Hilary to Poitiers, founded the hermitage at Ligugé, and was dragged, by popular acclaim, to the episcopal throne of Tours in 371.

## The Hermit Who Was a Bishop

For a quarter of a century Martin governed Tours while living like a monk. He built Marmoutier above the Loire and, with his monks, went out to evangelize the pagan countryside, cutting down temples and preaching in the market towns. His people loved him; his fellow bishops found him inconveniently holy. He died in 397 at Candes, "on fire with his old love of Christ," and the burial procession to Tours on 11 November became the saint's true feast day — the day of the first confessor, as the Church called him, one who had won the crown without the sword.
