Mary MacKillop was a pioneer in every sense — born at Fitzroy, Melbourne, in 1842, the first of eight children of Scottish immigrants who never quite managed to prosper. She went to work young, as a governess and then a teacher, and at twenty-four, with the parish priest Father Julian Woods, she founded something unheard of in the Australian bush: a congregation of sisters who would go where no one else would go, into the most remote settlements, to teach the poorest children. The Sisters of Saint Joseph of the Sacred Heart wore no great dowries and built no great motherhouses; they walked, and rode, and camped, carrying their schools into the outback in their saddlebags.

## The Servant of the Bush

In the early years her sisters lived with almost nothing — no fixed income, no monasteries, only the rule and the mission. MacKillop herself crossed the country on horseback and in sulkies, opening schools, orphanages, and refuges. She was tough and she was gentle, a redhead with a temper she mastered and a tenderness she gave to children no one else wanted. "We must not despise the poor," she wrote; "we must love them." By 1871 the order was spread across three colonies — and then the blow came that would test her faith to the root.

## Excommunicated and Vindicated

A misunderstanding with the bishop of Adelaide — he thought the sisters' obedience to their own superior deprived him of his authority — led to the most famous injustice of her life: in 1871 he excommunicated her, and she accepted it without a word of protest, telling her sisters only to "bear it in silence" and trust in God. Within a year he was satisfied, the excommunication was lifted, and she was restored — the vindication she had never asked for. She lived on to 1909, worn out, crippled, paralyzed by a stroke, still governing her order with her mind and her letters until the end. In 2010 Benedict XVI canonized her in Rome before a congregation that included Australian prime ministers and bush-folk alike — and the "saint of the bush," the teacher who had been excommunicated and forgiven, became the first Australian to be raised to the altars of the Church.
