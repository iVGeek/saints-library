Maximilian Kolbe was the great Catholic apostle of the twentieth century — a man of print and media, of the Immaculate Virgin, and finally of the death camps. Born in Poland in 1894, he entered the Franciscans young; a story of his boyhood tells of Mary appearing to him with two crowns, one white for purity and one red for martyrdom, and asking which he would take. He took both. He became a priest, a missionary to Japan, and the founder of Niepokalanów — a Franciscan monastery-city that was also a publishing house, printing millions of copies of its magazine, run entirely on faith and on the Immaculate.

## The City of the Immaculate

Niepokalanów was his masterwork: 700 brothers, a printing press, a radio station — a monastery as media empire, dedicated to Mary and to the spreading of her message. He had founded the Militia Immaculata as a young priest in Rome, a spiritual army for the conversion of souls; in Poland it grew into a movement. When war came in 1939, Kolbe turned the monastery into a refuge for Jews and Poles fleeing the invasion — as many as three thousand at a time. He was arrested, released, and arrested again, and in May 1941 the Gestapo sent him to Auschwitz as prisoner 16670.

## The Offering at Auschwitz

In July 1941 a prisoner escaped from Kolbe's block, and the camp commandant, in reprisal, selected ten men to be starved to death. One of them, Franciszek Gajowniczek, a sergeant and father, cried out for his wife and children. Kolbe stepped forward and asked to die in his place. The commandant, astonished, asked who he was; he answered simply. The ten were locked in the starvation bunker, and for two weeks Kolbe led the others in prayer and song until, one by one, they died. On 14 August 1941 he was killed by injection. Gajowniczek survived the war and stood at Kolbe's canonization in 1982, when John Paul II declared him a martyr of charity — "the patron of our difficult century" — proof that in the worst place the twentieth century could make, one man's love was still the strongest thing in the room.
