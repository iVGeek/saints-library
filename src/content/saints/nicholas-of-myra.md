Everyone knows Saint Nicholas as Santa Claus — but the real bishop of Myra was a wonderworker whose secret kindness outlived him by seventeen centuries. Born around 270 at Patara in Asia Minor, he inherited wealth, gave it all to the poor, and became bishop of the coastal city of Myra in the age of the last persecutions. He was imprisoned for the faith under Diocletian, released under Constantine, and sat at the Council of Nicaea in 325, where he was one of the eastern bishops who stood against Arius. The legends of his miracles — calming a storm at sea, restoring drowned sailors to life, saving three innocent men from execution — made him the great friend of sailors and travelers throughout the Mediterranean.

## The Secret Gifts

The story that became Santa Claus is the story of a father of three daughters with no dowries, about to sell them into slavery. Nicholas, hearing of the family's despair, threw a bag of gold through the window by night; then another; then a third — and when the father caught him the third time, Nicholas made him promise to tell no one. The bishop's anonymity, the bag of gold, the open window, the chimney of legend: all of it grows from a single act of hidden charity, and every child who finds a stocking filled on December 6 inherits the secret of a bishop who gave to God by giving to the poor.

## The Wonderworker and His Bones

The East honors Nicholas as "the Wonderworker," and his tomb at Myra was the great pilgrimage of the medieval East until 1087, when sailors of Bari carried his relics to Italy. There they built a basilica, and there the bones of the little bishop still rest — the same bones, so the tradition says, that once answered the prayers of sailors in storms. He is the patron of children, sailors, merchants, and pawnbrokers; Russia and Greece, Turkey and Norway all claim him; and on every December 6, in countries from the Netherlands to America, the world still remembers that a man who gives in secret is loved by God.
