The history of Ireland is inseparable from the story of one man: Patrick, a Roman Briton who was carried to Ireland as a slave and returned as a bishop, to bring the country the faith it has carried to the ends of the earth. His own writings — a short *Confession* and a letter — are among the earliest surviving works of British Christianity, and they let us hear his voice directly.

## Captivity and Conversion

Patrick was born about 387 in Roman Britain, the son of a deacon and grandson of a priest — a family nominally Christian, and one he later confessed he had neglected in his youth. At sixteen he was seized by Irish raiders and sold into slavery in Ireland, where he spent six years herding sheep, hungry and cold. It was there that he was converted. "I used to stay out in the forests and on the mountain," he wrote, "and I would wake up before daylight to pray in the snow and ice and rain." In a dream he was told that his ship was ready, and he escaped — walking two hundred miles to the sea and, after initial refusal, boarding a ship to freedom.

## The Voice of the Irish

Back in Britain he studied for the priesthood. But in another vision he heard "the voice of the Irish" — those who lived by the wood of Foclut, beyond the western sea, crying out, "We beg you, holy youth, come and walk among us once more." Around 432, now a bishop, Patrick returned to the land of his captivity. For thirty years he traveled Ireland, baptizing, ordaining clergy, founding churches and monasteries, and confronting the druids and kings. Tradition says he used the three-leaved shamrock to teach the mystery of the Trinity, drove the serpents from Ireland, and lit the Easter fire of Slane in defiance of the high king — the fire, it is said, that the kings could not extinguish.

## The Humble Saint

Patrick's own self-portrait is remarkably humble. He called himself "a sinner, the most unlearned of all the faithful," yet he writes with the joy of one who has seen God's mercy: "He that is mighty came and in His mercy lifted me up." He died on 17 March, probably in 461, at Saul, where he had built his first church. For sixteen centuries his feast has been kept throughout the world by the Irish diaspora, and the Church honors him as the patron of Ireland — a slave who became the Apostle of a people.
