Peter Damian was born poor and, in his own telling, half-starved in Ravenna around the year 1007 — the youngest child of a large family, orphaned early and put to tend hogs by a neglectful brother. A brother who was a priest rescued him, sent him to school, and watched him become one of the most learned men of his age. But learning was never his goal; holiness was. Around 1035 he entered the hermitage of Fonte Avellana, and in that remote house of prayer he found his true home.

## The Hermit in the Purple

From the hermitage, Peter's pen became a scourge. He wrote savagely — always out of love, he insisted — against the two scandals rotting the eleventh-century clergy: simony, the buying and selling of sacred office, and concubinage among priests. He begged popes to be severe, and when Pope Stephen IX made him cardinal-bishop of Ostia in 1057, he wept and protested, but he accepted, and the voice of the hermit was heard at last in the councils of the Church. As legate to Milan he restored order to a clergy that had bought their way into their posts; his treatises on the priesthood still burn with the fire of a man who believed the altar deserved clean hands.

## A Doctor of the Interior Life

Peter Damian is often remembered as a reformer, but the deeper current of his life ran in the other direction — toward silence. His *Liber Gomorrhianus* and his letters on monastic life are only half the story; the other half is the hermitage to which he always longed to return, the cell he called his "little Bethlehem," where he fasted, wept, and prayed for the Church he could not stop loving. The Church has honored both halves: he was named a Doctor of the Church in 1828, the title of one who teaches by pen — and, even more, by the pattern of a life spent in the presence of God.
