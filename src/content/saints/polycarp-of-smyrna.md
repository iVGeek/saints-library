Polycarp stands at the far end of the chain that links the Church to the apostles themselves. As a young man he had been formed by those who had seen Christ — Saint John the Evangelist above all — and the aged bishop of Smyrna was already a living monument of the apostolic age when, around the year 155, the empire demanded his death. His own disciples preserved an account of his martyrdom, the *Martyrdom of Polycarp*, which is among the most precious documents of the early Church.

## The Old Man Before the Proconsul

Arrested as the ringleader of the Christians, Polycarp was urged to save himself: "What harm is there in saying 'Caesar is Lord'?" But the old bishop would not. "Eighty-six years I have served Him," he answered, "and He has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King and my Savior?" The proconsul threatened him with wild beasts, with fire; Polycarp answered that the fire burns an hour and is quenched, while the fire of judgment is eternal. Brought to the stadium at Smyrna, he was condemned and bound to the stake. The flames, the account says, arched around him without touching him, and it was the executioner's sword that finished the work. His last recorded words were a prayer of thanksgiving — for every gift of a long life, offered back to God at its close.

## The Chain of Tradition

What makes Polycarp precious to the Church is not only his martyrdom but his placement in time. Through him we touch the generation after the apostles: he had heard John preach, and he in turn formed Irenaeus, who would defend the faith against the gnostic sects. His short letter to the Philippians still survives, a plain, warm exhortation to faith and charity, proof that the doctrine of the apostles was carried whole across the centuries in the memory of living men. When the Church honors the martyrs, it honors Polycarp as one of the first of its glories — the proof that the faith once delivered could be kept, and kept unto death.
