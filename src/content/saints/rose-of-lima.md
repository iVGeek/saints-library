Rose of Lima was the first saint born in the Americas — a girl whose beauty, her hagiographers insist, was so distracting that she took the name Rose and kept her face hidden behind flowers. She was born Isabel Flores de Oliva at Lima in 1586, the daughter of a soldier and a woman of indigenous descent; and from girlhood she showed a gift for prayer that set her apart. At twenty she took a vow of perpetual virginity and built herself a tiny hermitage in her parents' garden, where she spent her days in prayer and fasting, and her nights before a crucifix. She was not a nun — she never left her parents' house — and the Church calls her a virgin and a tertiary, a laywoman who lived the religious life without the habit or the cloister.

## The Flower of Lima

She did the work of a saint without leaving home. She wove lace and embroidered to buy food for the poor, and the rich of Lima sent their surplus to her so that she could distribute it. She nursed the sick of the city — and in a time when the hospitals were neglected and the poor died unattended, she brought them into her garden and tended them herself. The crown of thorns she wore beneath her veil was a secret between her and God; when her mother found it, she smiled and hid it again. The roses she is shown with in art are not merely decorative — they are the roses she grew in the garden where she met God, and the flowers with which she hid her face from the world.

## The First Saint of the New World

She died in 1617 at thirty-one, worn out by fasting and labor, and the whole city of Lima mourned her. She was canonized in 1671 by Clement X — the first native-born saint of the Americas — and her feast on August 23 became a national holiday in Peru. The garden hermitage became a shrine, and the roses that grew there became the symbol of a new kind of holiness: the saint who did not need a monastery or a mission field, only a garden, a crucifix, and a willingness to give everything to God in the quietest of places.
