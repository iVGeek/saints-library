The son of the mayor of York, England, an office his brother later held, early in life Robert began studying for the priesthood. He was ordained sub-deacon, but left his studies to become a Cistercian novice at the Newminster Abbey. Feeling a call to a life of holy solitude, he left the abbey and became a cave hermit on the River Nidd near Knaresborough, England. The cave, which had previously been occupied by a knight–hermit, had a chapel dedicated to Saint Giles.
After several years, a wealthy widow named Juliana offered Robert a hermit's cell at the Chapel of Saint Hilda in Rudfarlington, England. His reputation for wisdom and piety spread, and he developed a ministry to the poor, including redeeming men from debtor's prison. After about a year in Rudfarlington, his hermitage was destroyed by bandits. Robert lived for a while by the church of Spofforth, England, and then with some monks at Hedley, England; he found the life of the monks too easy, and returned to Rudfarlington.
Though he personally lived on a diet of herbs and roots and water, local benefactors gave him cattle and money, helped rebuild his hermitage, and paid for servants; Robert used all this largesse to help the poor. The combination of poor people and wealth led Sir William de Stuteville, constable of Knaresborough castle, to accuse Robert of harbouring thieves and bandits. Sir William destroyed the hermitage, claiming to be dispersing outlaws. Robert returned to his cave and lived there the rest of his life, healing pilgrims by prayer, and establishing an order of Trinitarian friars at Knaresborough Priory for all his would-be spiritual students.
