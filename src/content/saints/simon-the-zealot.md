Simon the Zealot is the most enigmatic of the apostles — a man whose name suggests he belonged to the Zealot movement of first-century Palestine, a political faction that sought the violent overthrow of Roman rule. If so, his conversion is one of the most radical in the New Testament: a revolutionary who exchanged the sword for the cross, and the cause of national liberation for the cause of the kingdom of God. We know almost nothing else of him from the Gospels, except that he was one of the Twelve and that he was present at the Last Supper and at Pentecost.

## The Apostle of the East

After Pentecost, tradition takes Simon on the great apostolic mission to the East — to Egypt, North Africa, Persia, and possibly Britain, where he preached alongside Jude Thaddaeus and founded churches that would survive for centuries. The early tradition says he was martyred in Persia, some accounts saying he was sawn in half — the same instrument of death, legend says, that the apostle had once used as a tool of his trade before the call of Christ. Other traditions place his martyrdom in Britain, where he is said to have preached in the farthest reaches of the Roman world.

## The Quiet Revolutionary

What makes Simon's story important to the Church is the silence of his name. He is never quoted in the Gospels, never given a speech in the Acts, never credited with a miracle — and yet he is counted among the Twelve, one of the innermost circle of the Lord. The Church remembers him not for what he said but for what he was: a man who was something else before Christ, and who let Christ make him into something new. The Zealot became a saint; the revolutionary became a disciple; and the quiet apostle of the East still reminds us that the most faithful are not always the loudest, and that the greatest conversions are often the ones we know nothing about.
