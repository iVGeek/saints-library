Teresa of Ávila is one of the great women of history by any measure — founder, reformer, mystic, and writer whose place in Spanish literature is beside Cervantes. She was born in 1515 into a pious Castilian family, entered the Carmelites at twenty against her father's protests, and for years lived the easy, worldly life of a sixteenth-century convent — friends, confidences, comforts. Then, around 1560, at a moment of crisis she came upon an image of the wounded Christ and fell at His feet; the love that overcame her that day never left her, and the woman who had been comfortable became the fire that burned across Spain.

## The Reformer

She decided that her order must be reformed — returned to the primitive Carmelite rule of prayer, poverty, and silence — and in 1562 she founded the convent of St. Joseph at Ávila with a handful of nuns, against the united opposition of the city and her own superiors. From that small house grew the Discalced Carmelites: seventeen convents of women and fifteen of men, the latter begun when she recruited a young friar named John of the Cross to her cause. The opposition was ferocious — she was denounced, sued, and even threatened with the Inquisition — and Teresa met it all with the wit and courage of a Castilian grandee's daughter, writing letters by the hundreds and riding the roads of Spain in mule carts until she was old and broken.

## The Teacher of Prayer

Her books came from her own soul. The Autobiography, the Way of Perfection, the Interior Castle — the last written in a few months in 1577, mapping the soul's journey inward through the seven mansions to the center where God dwells — are the masterpieces of Catholic mysticism. She had a phrase for everything: prayer is "nothing but friendly conversation with Him who loves us"; the recollection of the soul is a castle of crystal with God at its heart. She died in 1582 at Alba de Tormes, saying "O my Lord and my Spouse, the hour has come," and within forty years she was a saint, canonized in the same 1622 ceremony as Ignatius, Xavier, and Philip Neri. In 1970 Paul VI made her the first woman Doctor of the Church — proof, to use her own words, that "God has always given women the grace to be brave."
