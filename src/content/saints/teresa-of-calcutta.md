Mother Teresa of Calcutta — canonized in 2016 as Saint Teresa of Calcutta — was one of the most famous women of the twentieth century, yet she always insisted that her work was simply what Christ asked of any disciple: to serve him in the poorest of the poor. "I am a little pencil in the hand of a writing God, who is sending a love letter to the world," she said.

## The Call Within a Call

She was born Agnes Gonxha Bojaxhiu in 1910 in Skopje, to Albanian parents. At eighteen she joined the Loreto Sisters, taking the name Teresa, and sailed for India. For nearly two decades she taught geography and catechism to the girls of Saint Mary's School in Calcutta. Then, on 10 September 1946, on a train to Darjeeling for retreat, she received what she called her "call within a call": to leave the security of the convent school and live among the poorest of the poor, becoming Christ's presence to them. She described it simply: "I was to leave the convent and work with the poor, living among them."

## The Missionaries of Charity

In 1948 she exchanged her Loreto habit for the white sari with the blue border that became her habit, and began gathering the dying from the streets of Calcutta. In 1950 the Missionaries of Charity were established; they now serve on every continent. In 1952 she opened Nirmal Hriday ("Place of the Pure Heart"), a home for the dying where the abandoned could die with dignity — a dignity she described as "a beautiful death, like Jesus on the cross." Over the decades she founded homes for orphans, lepers, the mentally ill, and the unwanted, and took her message of respect for every human life to the world's centers of power.

## The Saint in the Darkness

Her later years were marked by a hidden trial: a long, painful "dark night" of the soul, in which she felt the absence of God while continuing to serve him without consolation. Her letters, published after her death, revealed that beneath her radiant public smile she carried a profound interior suffering — which made her holiness all the more real. She died in Calcutta on 5 September 1997. Pope John Paul II beatified her with extraordinary speed in 2003, and Pope Francis canonized her in 2016. "She often said, 'My God, how I love you,'" the Pope said at her canonization — "and she lived it."
