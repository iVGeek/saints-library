Thomas Becket began as the king's man, and died as the Church's. Born in London in 1118, the son of a Norman merchant, he rose through talent and charm to become the most dazzling figure in England after the king: the learned clerk, the man of business, the wit and hunter and companion of Henry II. In 1154 the king made him Chancellor, and the two friends ran England together — magnificent, ruthless, inseparable. And then Henry, looking to bring the Church to heel, decided to make his brilliant friend archbishop of Canterbury. It was, Henry said later, the worst mistake of his life.

## The Courtier Who Became a Saint

The consecration changed him overnight. The chancellor who had ridden with falcons put on a hair shirt; the friend of the king's table began to eat alone and rise for vigils. When Henry demanded, in the Constitutions of Clarendon, that bishops and clergy be subject to royal courts, Becket refused — and the king, in fury, drove him out. For six years the archbishop lived in exile in France, poor and harried; and when a nominal reconciliation brought him home to Canterbury in 1170, he returned like a man coming to his own battlefield. On Christmas Day he preached from the pulpit on the text "Peace I leave with you," and then, to a stunned congregation, he announced: "There is a martyr coming."

## The Murder in the Cathedral

Four knights, hearing the king's cry that no one would rid him of the turbulent priest, rode to Canterbury and burst into the cathedral as Vespers was being sung. They found Becket in the transept, before the altar of Saint Benedict. He refused to flee. He met them at the pillar, wrapped in his archiepiscopal vestments, and gave himself up: "I am ready to die for my God, for the honor of the Church." The first blow fell; he fell, praying, and the second stroke — as the monks would testify — smashed the crown of his head. Within three years he was a saint, and Canterbury became the shrine at which all England prayed — the pilgrimage of Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*, where the martyr's tomb was the great goal of the Middle Ages. The king who destroyed him had unwittingly given the Church of England its most unbreakable memory: a shepherd who would not bend, and died for it at his own altar.
