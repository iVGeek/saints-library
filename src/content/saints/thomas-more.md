Thomas More rose through the law courts of London to the highest office in England, wrote a book that still names a political ideal, and then walked to his death with a joke on his lips rather than swear an oath he believed false. He was born in 1478, trained as a lawyer, and in his youth lived among the Carthusians at the London Charterhouse, weighing the priesthood before choosing marriage and family. The two loves — the cloister and the hearth — never left him; his house in Chelsea was a school of piety where prayers were said daily in common and his daughter Margaret studied Greek.

## The Chancellor and the King

More's learning made him the darling of the European humanists, and his good sense made him invaluable to Henry VIII, who raised him to Lord Chancellor in 1529. But when the king's divorce and the break with Rome forced a choice, More chose conscience. He resigned in 1532, refused the Oath of Supremacy, and was shut in the Tower for a year, writing letters to his daughter on the art of dying well. Brought to trial, he could not be convicted by any evidence of disloyal words — only by perjury — and he was condemned for his silence. On the scaffold he declared himself "the King's good servant, but God's first."

## A Saint for the Public Square

More's greatness is not that he fled the world but that he lived in it with such wholeness — lawyer, judge, statesman, father, jester, and contemplative in one person. The Church honors him not as a courtier who fell but as a layman who showed that holiness belongs in chancellories as surely as in cloisters. That is why Saint John Paul II named him patron of statesmen and politicians, and why his feast, kept with John Fisher on June 22, is a standing reminder that the integrity of a public life is measured, in the end, by the truth one will not sell.
