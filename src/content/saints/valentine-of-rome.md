Two Christian Valentines of the third century share the same name and the same glory — a priest of Rome and a bishop of Terni, both martyred, both honored on February 14. History has never sorted their stories cleanly apart, and the Church, remembering that their heroic deeds "are known to God alone," keeps them together under one feast. What is certain is that a Roman priest named Valentine died for Christ in the persecution of Claudius Gothicus around the year 269, and that Christians have honored his tomb on the Via Flaminia ever since.

## The Priest Who Blessed Loves

The legends woven around Valentine speak less of doctrine than of kindness: a priest who married Christian couples in secret when marriage was forbidden, who befriended his jailer and restored sight to his jailer's blind daughter, who left a note signed "Your Valentine" — so runs the charming tradition behind the sending of cards. History cannot certify the details, but the Church's instinct is sound: it set the feast of a martyr of love in the middle of February, at the threshold of spring, and in doing so it blessed the human impulse that had run for centuries on the pagan festival of Lupercalia.

## A Christian Answer to Romance

Modern lovers celebrate Valentine without knowing they are keeping a martyr's feast. The Church's reading of the day is richer: the love of a man and a woman is holy because it images the love of God, and it is worth dying for — as the martyrs proved — not dying over. The heart of Saint Valentine is not the paper heart of the greeting-card but the pierced heart of a Christian witness, offered with his life, so that love on earth might be guarded by love from heaven.
